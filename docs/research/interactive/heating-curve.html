It then compares the result with the SAT heating-curve formula, the boiler's 4.3 kW minimum output and the underfloor mixer's needs.

What to notice

  • The curve is not a straight line. Because radiator output rises with ΔT^1.3, the needed flow temperature drops quickly at first and then flattens. That is why commercial curves (and SAT's formula) are slightly bent.
  • Bigger radiators mean lower temperatures. Drop "sized for flow at design" from 70 to 55 °C, which is the same as having larger radiators, and the return stays in the condensing zone even on the coldest day.
  • The red zone is where short cycling lives. Above the outdoor temperature where the house needs less than 4.3 kW, the boiler cannot turn down far enough. It has to switch on and off, and the thermostat should manage that deliberately (chapter 05).
  • The floor sets a floor. A thermostatic underfloor mixer can only mix down. While the floor is heating, the boiler flow must stay above the mixer setting, which on mild days is higher than the radiators need.
  • Fit the curve, then trim with the room sensor. Move the SAT coefficient until the blue line sits on the orange one: that is roughly the value a self-learning curve would converge to. The room-sensor PI trim corrects what remains (sun, wind, people).

Steady-state physics only: no solar or internal gains, so real needed temperatures are a little lower; heat loss is assumed proportional to indoor − outdoor temperature. The default −13 °C design temperature is a common Hungarian design value; ask the installer for the one used for your house. Sources: EN 442 radiator law, SAT heating_curve.py; see chapter 04.
